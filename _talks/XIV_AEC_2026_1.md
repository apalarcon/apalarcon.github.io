# Abstact
Tropical easterly waves (TEWs) are fundamental phenomena in the tropical North Atlantic that influence precipitation and serve as precursors to tropical cyclones. This research performs a comprehensive analysis of the moisture sources that fuel their precipitation in the current and future climate. Our methodology integrates a curvature vorticity tracking algorithm with Lagrangian moisture source diagnostics, applied to a dynamically downscaled ERA5 dataset for the historical analysis (1985–2023) and a bias-corrected CMIP6 dataset for future projections. The resulting climatology identifies a yearly average of 41 ± 5 TEWs, which travel westward at approximately 8 m/s with activity concentrated in the July–September period. We found that the origin of moisture for these systems evolves as they cross the basin: initial precipitation is fed by sources over Africa and the adjacent ocean, while waves in the western Atlantic increasingly rely on moisture transported over long distances by the easterly winds. Notably, our historical analysis did not detect any statistically significant long-term increase in their 6-hourly area average moisture uptake or precipitation. However,  despite a stable TEW frequency, future projections signal a significant intensification of wet TEWs. Wet TEWs are projected to become considerably wetter, particularly in the western tropical North Atlantic. By the end of the century (2071-2100) under the SSP5-8.5 scenario, this intensification reaches scaling rates of approximately 8.5%/°C for moisture uptake and 4.0%/°C for precipitation. Overall, this work provides new quantitative evidence that while the number of TEWs may not change, their capacity to produce extreme rainfall events is likely to increase in a warming world.
